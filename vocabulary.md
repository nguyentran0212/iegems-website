This page is a glossary of the words I use in reviews, plus a short background primer for readers who are new to audio. The vocabulary is organised by what each term describes: timbre and tonality, dynamics and bass, resolution and detail, soundstage and spatial attributes, and the source chain for DAC / amp / DAP reviews.

Where my vocabulary overlaps with the [Sound Wheel lexicon](https://www.itu.int/pub/R-REP-BS.2399) from ITU-R BS.2399, both terms are kept, with a cross-reference between them. The Sound Wheel terms come first inside each group.

## Background

Audio reproduction is a chain of small steps. A recording is made. A DAC turns its bits into an analog voltage. An amplifier makes that voltage loud enough. An IEM driver pushes the air in your ear canal. Your eardrum bends. Your brain hears sound.

None of this is mysterious. Every step is engineering, and every step can be measured or described. The reason IEMs are interesting is not that they break physics. They cannot. The reason they are interesting is that small engineering decisions at each step (the choice of driver material, the way a crossover is wired, the impedance of the amplifier, the frequency response of the IEM as a filter) produce audible differences.

The engineering / tuning distinction is worth holding clear. The **tuning** of an IEM is the frequency response the engineer picks: which frequencies to emphasise, which to subdue. The **engineering** is everything else that makes the IEM musical or boring: how cleanly the driver starts and stops transients, how it behaves under load, how it controls resonances, how it pairs with your amp's impedance. "Musical" lives in tuning. "Technical" lives in engineering. Good IEMs have both.

The **frequency response** is the IEM's tuning, rendered as a graph. It tells you how loud the IEM is at each frequency, from 20Hz (subbass) to 20kHz (upper treble). It encodes most of what I grade on. It does not capture everything: stereo imaging, transient response, the way an IEM behaves in a busy mix. When the graph and my impression disagree, I get curious about which is wrong.

When I review an IEM, I am after **immersion**: the orchestra around my head, every instrument pannable in space, near or far, never flat. What I grade on is what I call **technical performance**, an umbrella for everything in the listening experience that is not preference. Tonality is preference; technical performance is not. The four dimensions I rate on are **Timbre and Tonality**, **Bass and Dynamic**, **Resolution**, and **Soundstage and Imaging**. The full rubrics live on the [methodology](/methodology) page; the vocabulary below is the language I use inside those dimensions.

The best IEMs I have heard behave like a calibrated display: faithful reproduction is the purpose, the engineer bends the rules gently to improve the image. They **disappear**: you forget they are in your ears, and you hear only what was recorded. That is the standard. The terms below are how I talk about how close an IEM gets.

## Timbre and tonality

Tone is the relative loudness and timbre of different elements in a mix. Tone shapes what an IEM emphasises. I keep the rating conservative on this dimension because tonality is largely a matter of preference; a well-judged warmer or brighter presentation is fine if it is delivered without sacrificing the precision of the other dimensions.

**Harsh (or harshness).** An aggression in the upper-midrange or lower treble that makes cymbals, sibilant recordings, or bright vocals sound painful rather than energetic. *What it sounds like: a slight edge when someone says "s" or a cymbal is hit; audible at moderate volume.*

**Sibilance.** The overemphasis of "s" and "t" sounds in vocals, bright cymbals, and similar high-frequency content. *What it sounds like: an excess of "s" sounds in vocals, especially in tracks like Ed Sheeran's Shivers.*

**Dry.** A timbre that emphasises the attack and fundamental of a note, with reduced harmonics and reverb. *What it sounds like: a violin that sounds clean, present, and unromantic, more like in the recording booth than a concert hall.*

**Smooth.** The opposite of dry. A timbre with rounded edges and gentle harmonics, often at the cost of detail. *What it sounds like: easy to listen to and fatigue-free, but with less micro-detail.*

**Full.** A timbre that sounds dense and complete across the frequency range. *What it sounds like: nothing missing from the picture; instruments feel real.*

**Bright.** A treble-heavy balance that emphasises detail, air, and upper harmonics. *What it sounds like: cymbals and hats are forward; the IEM has energy.*

**Dark.** A treble-shy balance; the opposite of bright. *What it sounds like: upper-frequency detail feels recessed; cymbals get lost behind vocals.*

**Textured.** A timbre that reveals the surface detail of an instrument (the wood grain in a violin, the air in a flute). *What it sounds like: instruments feel like they have a "skin," not a generic midrange smoothness.*

**Warm.** A midrange and lower-midrange-forward balance. *What it sounds like: vocals sound fuller and closer; bass has body.*

**Cold (or cool).** A midrange and treble-forward balance with leaner bass. *What it sounds like: precise, analytical, sometimes thin.*

**Neutral.** A balance that does not obviously emphasise any frequency region. *What it sounds like: nothing obviously "wrong" with the tonal balance, though it can still sound subtly coloured.*

**Veiled (or veil).** The sensation that the midrange is masked or covered by lower-midrange emphasis, reducing detail and clarity without obvious harshness or peak. *What it sounds like: instruments sound slightly muffled; details are there but require extra effort to hear. Common cause: lower-midrange shelf from a 200 to 500Hz bump. Related to the Sound Wheel's "homogeneous" and "full," but specifically a diagnostic about emphasis rather than a description.*

**Bite (or bitey).** Upper-midrange emphasis that gives voices and instruments a sense of presence, edge, and forward momentum. *What it sounds like: vocals feel just in front of the music; cymbals have an incisive edge. Related to the Sound Wheel's "presence" and "clarity."*

**V-shape.** A tuning with both bass and treble lifted relative to the midrange, forming a "V" on the frequency response graph. *What it sounds like: bass and treble are emphasised; the midrange feels recessed. Common in earlier IEM designs like the Shure SE215.*

**U-shape.** Similar to V-shape, but with the bass and treble lifts gentler and the midrange less obviously recessed. *What it sounds like: a balanced and engaging sound that still has body in the midrange.*

**W-shape.** A tuning with bass, treble, and upper-midrange all lifted, with two dips in between. *What it sounds like: punchy bass and articulate vocals together, sometimes at the cost of midrange naturalness.*

**L-shape.** Bass-heavy with relatively flat midrange and treble. *What it sounds like: rhythm-forward, less attention-grabbing in vocals.*

**Warm-neutral.** A balance close to neutral but with a small touch of bass or lower-midrange. *What it sounds like: clean and uncoloured overall, but slightly full rather than lean.*

**Dead room.** A recording or reproduction that lacks reverb, air, and ambient cues. *What it sounds like: instruments sound isolated, as if in a heavily-treated studio with no sense of the room they were recorded in.*

## Dynamics and bass

Dynamics are the loudness swings in the music and how the IEM handles them. Bass includes the low-frequency region of the spectrum, not just its loudness.

**Attack.** How quickly a note reaches full intensity after the driver starts moving. *What it sounds like: a drum hit that lands all at once, with a sharp "crack" rather than a soft "thump."*

**Bass precision.** How cleanly bass notes are defined, separated from each other, and held in time. *What it sounds like: a busy bassline where you can follow individual notes rather than a blur of sound.*

**Punch.** The physical sensation of a beat in the eardrums or chest. *What it sounds like: kick drums and bass guitar feel like a clean hit.*

**Powerful.** A wider dynamic range and intensity headroom, especially in the bass. *What it sounds like: the music has room to breathe, and loud passages get loud without distortion.*

**Weak.** The opposite of powerful; bass with no body, dynamics compressed. *What it sounds like: drums feel like they're at one volume with no growth.*

**Snappy.** Bass attacks that are fast, decisive, and clean. The bass note starts and stops on time without lingering or smearing. *What it sounds like: a tight punch rather than a pillowy impact, the way a real kick drum sounds. Related to the Sound Wheel's "attack" and "bass precision," with a stronger connotation of quickness.*

**Oomph.** The physical, visceral sensation of bass impact, especially felt in the chest or throat. *What it sounds like: not just "you hear the bass," but "you feel the bass." Related to the Sound Wheel's "punch" and "powerful," with a stronger connotation of felt weight.*

**Grippy (or grippiness).** Bass that has texture and grip, not just thump-thump. *What it sounds like: bass lines have a growl and elasticity; individual notes are felt as separate impacts rather than a single mass. Related to the Sound Wheel's "textured" (timbre sense) and "natural," applied to bass specifically.*

**Toe-tapping.** Whether the IEM makes you want to tap along to the rhythm. *What it sounds like: the music has a sense of drive and momentum, defined by whether the IEM conveys rhythm well enough to make you move.*

**Mushy.** Bass that lacks definition, with notes blurring into each other. *What it sounds like: bass impacts are soft, slow, and indistinct.*

**Boomy.** Bass with excess midbass that lingers and bleeds into the lower midrange. *What it sounds like: notes feel large and resonant but uncontrolled; the IEM feels bass-heavy in an unenjoyable way.*

**Hollow.** Midrange that sounds empty or distant, often from a 200 to 500Hz dip. *What it sounds like: vocals feel like they're surrounded by space and not centred.*

**Pillowy.** Bass that is soft and poofy rather than tight. *What it sounds like: each beat feels like a fluffy impact rather than a precise punch.*

**Whip-crack.** A vivid comparison for snappy bass: a short, sharp, fast attack. *What it sounds like: a clean whip breaking through air. Used as a reference point rather than a literal sound.*

## Resolution and detail

Resolution is the IEM's ability to keep elements of a mix distinct and to render fine details and decay-side textures.

**Clean.** A presentation that is clear and well-defined, with no smearing or muddiness. *What it sounds like: every element of the mix is distinguishable.*

**Presence.** A sense that the singer or instrument is in the room with you. *What it sounds like: vocals feel like they're standing just in front of the music.*

**Clarity.** The IEM's ability to keep the elements of a mix distinct and easy to follow. *What it sounds like: instruments do not bleed into each other; the music is easy to track.*

**Detailed.** The IEM's ability to reveal micro-detail, the small things in the recording that you'd otherwise miss. *What it sounds like: subtle things like a bow catching a violin string, a singer's breath before a phrase, or reverb tails.*

**Natural.** A timbre that does not impose its own character on the music. *What it sounds like: instruments sound like themselves, not like they're being filtered through some signature.*

**Macro-resolution.** The IEM's ability to keep elements of a complex mix distinguishable. *What it sounds like: in busy or layered recordings, you can still identify each part without losing track.*

**Micro-resolution.** The IEM's ability to reveal fine details and nuances within individual parts of the mix. *What it sounds like: subtle things like reverb tails, breath, and decay are audible.*

**Separation.** The IEM's ability to prevent elements of a mix from overlapping and mushing together. *What it sounds like: each instrument has its own space; nothing crowds anything else.*

**Decay.** The end-side of a note, after the attack: how a note fades out. *What it sounds like: a piano note with all its reverb tail intact, or a cymbal that sustains and tapers naturally.*

**Transients.** The fast, large jumps in loudness at the beginning and end of notes: the attack and decay combined. *What it sounds like: drums and plucked strings that feel alive and not smoothed over.*

## Soundstage and spatial attributes

The soundstage is the perceived field of sound around the listener. Imaging is the precision of placement within it. Both depend on the recording and on the IEM.

**Width.** How far left and right the soundstage extends. *What it sounds like: in stereo recordings, instruments and vocals are placed across a wider stage; sometimes the sound extends past your ears.*

**Depth.** How far front-to-back the soundstage extends. *What it sounds like: near and far elements are distinguishable in a mix.*

**Envelopment.** The sensation that the sound surrounds you rather than coming from two points. *What it sounds like: ambient information from the recording wraps around the head.*

**Localisation.** The ability to pinpoint where a sound is in space, particularly left-to-right. *What it sounds like: you can tell exactly where each instrument is in the stereo image, with high precision.*

**Distance.** How far away a sound source appears to be in the mix. *What it sounds like: a backing vocal at 20 feet rather than two feet.*

**Internality.** Whether sound appears inside your head or outside, in the listening space. *What it sounds like: with low internality, sounds feel external and you can imagine speakers in front of you. With high internality, everything is between your ears.*

**Reverberation.** The ambient decay of the recording space. *What it sounds like: a recording with reverb sounds like it is happening in a hall; a dry recording sounds like it happened in a closet.*

**Layering.** The illusion that elements in a mix are stacked at multiple depths, from closer to further away. *What it sounds like: a complex orchestral recording where individual sections feel like they sit in different planes rather than all on one wide but flat surface. Related to the Sound Wheel's "depth" and "localisation," with a stronger focus on the multiple-stacks image.*

**3D vs 2D.** A shorthand for whether the soundstage has depth and layering (3D) or only width (2D). *What it sounds like: a 3D IEM paints a small dome around the head with elements in front, behind, above, and to the sides; a 2D IEM gives wide left-to-right imaging but elements feel like they're on a flat plane between the ears. Related to the Sound Wheel's "spatial extent" plus "depth" plus "localisability" combined.*

**Holographic.** The illusion that sound comes from outside the head, like external speakers. *What it sounds like: IEMs with top-tier spatial performance occasionally trick you into thinking the sound comes from your room. Matches the Sound Wheel's description.*

**Pin-point (precision).** Very precise localisation; the source is exactly where it appears to be. *What it sounds like: in a complex mix, you can tell that a sound is, say, 30 degrees left and 15 degrees up, not just "somewhere to the left." Related to the Sound Wheel's "localisability."*

**In-head vs out-of-head.** Whether sounds appear inside the head (in-head) or in the listening space around you (out-of-head). *What it sounds like: out-of-head IEMs feel like the sound extends into the room; in-head IEMs feel like the sound is all inside the skull. Same as the Sound Wheel's "internality."*

**Near-to-far.** The distance gradient in a soundstage, from close sound sources to distant ones. *What it sounds like: a track with both foreground and background elements where the depth from close to far is well-rendered. Related to the Sound Wheel's "distance."*

## Source chain (DAC, amp, DAP)

These terms come up in DAC, headphone amplifier, and digital audio player reviews. The chain works like this: source (recorded audio) → DAC → amp → driver → your ear. Each link shapes what you hear.

**DAC.** A digital-to-analog converter. The part of the chain that turns recording bits into an analog voltage. *What it does: defines most of the source's character (tonal balance, output impedance, noise floor).*

**Amp (or amplifier).** The part of the chain that boosts the DAC's voltage to a level the driver needs. *What it does: defines output power, current delivery, and how the source behaves with different IEM impedances.*

**DAP (Digital Audio Player).** A portable, battery-powered device that combines a DAC, an amp, storage, and a screen. *What it does: lets you carry your music library and listening chain in your pocket.*

**Dongle (or USB DAC).** A small, often cable-attached DAC and amp combo that plugs into a phone or computer via USB. *What it does: a portable source for IEM listening when the host device lacks a good output.*

**Output power.** How much electrical power the amp can deliver to the IEM, measured in milliwatts at a rated impedance (e.g., 100mW at 32Ω). *What it tells you: whether the amp can drive harder IEMs to loud, undistorted volume.*

**Output impedance.** The internal impedance of the amp's output. *Why it matters: if too high relative to the IEM's impedance, the frequency response of the IEM shifts. The rule of thumb is to keep output impedance at or below 1/8th of the IEM's impedance.*

**Sensitivity (of an IEM).** How loud the IEM plays for a given power input, expressed in dB/mW or dB/Vrms. *What it tells you: easier to drive when sensitivity is higher.*

**Impedance (of an IEM).** The IEM's electrical resistance to the amp's signal, in ohms. *What it tells you: lower-impedance IEMs are usually easier to drive but more sensitive to output-impedance variation.*

**Current delivery.** How much current the amp can supply at peak transients. *Why it matters: large bass transients need lots of current; an amp with weak current delivery sounds fine at low volumes but strained on bass-heavy music.*

**Driveability (or drivability).** Whether the IEM sounds good from the source you have. *What it sounds like: an IEM that is hard to drive sounds muffled, congested, and bass-light from a weak amp; the same IEM sounds full, snappy, and 3D from a stronger amp. The reverse, sensitivity to hiss from a powerful source, also counts.*

**Gain.** The amp's volume step-up, expressed in dB. *Why it matters: low-gain amps make sensitive IEMs hiss-free but limit maximum volume on hard-to-drive IEMs.*

**Scaling.** Whether an IEM sounds noticeably better with a stronger amp. *What it sounds like: a scalable IEM feels like a wall separating foreground and background comes down when you swap to a stronger amp; the soundstage opens up, the bass tightens, the dynamics expand.*

**Pitch-black background.** A source with so little noise or hiss that quiet passages are truly quiet. *What it sounds like: in a quiet recording or during a silent moment, you hear absolute silence rather than faint hiss.*

**Balanced vs single-ended.** Two output topologies. Single-ended uses one signal wire plus ground (3.5mm, 6.35mm). Balanced uses two signal wires plus ground (often 2.5mm or 4.4mm). *Why it matters: balanced outputs generally have higher power, less crosstalk, and can be more quiet.*

**DAC filter (or treble filter).** A small, user-selectable filter on some DACs that rolls off or sharpens the upper treble slightly. *Why it matters: a subtle but real difference in treble presentation; some listeners prefer one filter over another for treble-sensitive recordings.*

**MQA.** A lossy compression format that folds high-resolution audio into a smaller file. *What it does: a complex encoding scheme that lets you store high-res audio inside what looks like a CD-quality file. Decoding requires a hardware or software MQA decoder.*

**LDAC, AAC, aptX, SBC, UAT.** Bluetooth audio codecs. *How they differ: LDAC and UAT support higher bitrates and better audio quality; AAC is common on Apple devices; aptX is on many Android devices; SBC is the lowest common denominator.*

**Bit depth and sample rate.** Specifications that define how precisely the digital audio captures the original analog signal. Common values: 16-bit / 44.1kHz (CD quality), 24-bit / 96kHz (high resolution). *Note: human hearing tops out well below 24-bit / 96kHz precision; the value of higher resolution is mostly about preserving headroom for mixing and mastering, not for playback.*
