# AFUL Performer 8S assessment

## Listening Test

### Shivers by Ed Sheeran

https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=z2_Lrg6rRks

Zero harshness or sibilance. At the same time, it is not muted or feeling low resolution in the nuance details conveyed by the higher frequencies. The side-channel backing vocals and the small details and breaths in Ed's voice are present. Ed's voice has an emotive warmth and richness to it. It sits at the upper edge of what I consider neutral-warm. If it had a touch more energy in the lower mid, I would start complaining. In the current state, it is just right and a rather enjoyable tone.

### Kiwi wa Boku ni Niteiru by See-Saw

https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=U6BkF23UPbM

The voice is bright, vibrant and clear without being harsh or edgy. The neutral-warm midrange gives the voice a musical richness that is just nice to listen to. What stands out is the nuances and details from the background and side channels popping up behind the main voice. The overdub on this track is a particular joy to listen to. The mix has a very good 3D sensation, with elements distributing clearly from left to right and low to high. Some higher sounds appear to float above my head in the instrumental part around 3:30.

### Now We Are Free by 2CELLOS

https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=74CYIdYoQ5w

The sense of ambience is excellent. The female vocal chant in the first 30 seconds does not project far into the surrounding room, but it gives a very clear sense of localisation, like the voice hovers somewhere outside, front right, slightly above my head. This out-of-head sensation persists in many parts of the track. The cellos tone is great across the range. The Performer 8S produces the resonance and rumble of the low notes and maintains a proper tone in the middle range without sounding thin. If I were to nitpick, I prefer a bit less lower midrange on this track. The warmth can make the cellos tone sound slightly muffled in some parts. A small issue, but it confirms that the Performer 8S is not a fully neutral-tuned IEM. It is slightly warm, which works great on some tracks and causes small issues on others. Nothing deal-breaking, tonally.

### Playing God by Polyphia

https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Z5NoQg8LdDk

Tim Henson's main guitar is clear but not harsh. There is an enjoyable warmth imbued on the guitar tone, but not overbearing or muffled. Cymbals and hi-hats have clear details: full details and decay of the cymbal crash, and distinct hits on the hats. The cymbals and hats are not harsh, though they could not stand out enough for some listeners. Personally, the balance is just right. The bass guitar has good but not overbearing presence. Focusing on the bass guitar reveals a lot of details in the notes, not just low-pitched hum. The kick drums feel very impactful. The attacks are clean, with authoritative physical punch at the top of the note. A very nice drum rendition.

### Bach's Goldberg Variations (Lang Lang)

https://youtu.be/linQUK6jmg

The piano is quite balanced. High notes have proper crispness that cuts through the mix. At the same time, the left hand is easy to follow and not overpowered by the high notes. I can sit back and let my mind follow all the voices in the piece at the same time and feel how they interplay with each other. If I have one complaint, I would say that the overall tone across the board is slightly warm. Not alarming, and some listeners would be happy with the warmth, but it reads more as artistic rule-bending than a strictly correct rendition. Side note: comparing the Tidal (24-bit / 96 kHz) version against the YouTube version of the same track, the Tidal version sounds more detailed and the stage feels a bit more airy and spacious. The difference is more audible than usual on this IEM. At least redbook 44.1 kHz is recommended as a source quality floor for the Performer 8S.

### Ciaccona from Bach's Violin Partita No. 2 in D Minor (Kavakos)

https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=AtA5DbswJ90

No harshness, and an impressive level of details. The breath of Kavakos is audible. The sound of the violin bouncing off and decaying in the recording hall accompanies the main violin tone. The tone of the violin is a bit warmer than what I usually expect, and the nuance scratchiness of the violin is not as exposed as I usually expect, probably as a result of the warmth. I can see people enjoying this slightly more musical tone over the straight play of a neutral and highly detailed IEM like the AFUL Cantor. Do not mistake this for lacking details though. It has all the details that would satisfy me in careful, deliberate listening sessions. The dynamic variation this IEM pulls out is also a joy, which is critical for a successful rendition of a master performance of the Ciaccona like this one by Kavakos.

### Synchro BOM-BA-YE by Tokyo Kosei Wind Orchestra

https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=4lwPiXnOGZ4

The Performer 8S passes the hand claps test well. Nothing unnatural or uncomfortable. The claps are crisp, detailed, and have a good sense of resonance in the recording. The layering of foreground instruments over the bass instruments is particularly good. The bass brass is easy to hear on attention, and the bass is not just a hum but has details. All the brass and woodwind have plenty of details when I pay attention, but the details are not in-your-face. There is a slightly warm tone across the recording, making it more musical and gentle. The energy level may be a bit muted or reduced compared to a straight-play neutral IEM, which could be a con for listeners after a more bright and energetic sound. On the other hand, the bass and drums are authoritative, and the sense of beat and rhythm is excellent.

### A Reckoning in Blood (Ghost of Tsushima OST)

https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=kaBMXXo-vdU

The crescendo at 01:10. Cannot stop grinning and feeling blood pumping when it drops. The Performer 8S does a great job with the dynamic here. Every beat has excellent control on the attack side, but the Performer 8S does not skimp on the energy at the top of the beat or the decay of the bass note to make the attack crisper. It is a very authoritative drum rendition, not in the sense of "good for a neutral IEM," just downright great bass and drum.

### Strength of a Thousand Men (Live) by Two Steps from Hell

https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=POmTXTBtRZA

Another outstanding bass performance. The bass line at the beginning has a clear rumble, and the bass notes have a clear sense of texture, feeling grippy. The Performer 8S shows excellent control here. The sense of ambience is also great, with the background vocal chants feeling like they float up on my side, slightly outside my head. The female vocal chant at 3:20 is in the center, floating slightly above on the stage, with good presence to cut through the mix. In the busier sections in the middle and near the end, everything feels a bit too clumped together. I am not sure if it is because of the warmer tone getting in the way, or if it is the performance ceiling of the Performer 8S.

### Ciaccona from Bach's Violin Partita No. 2 in D Minor (Kavakos) — Resolution pass

https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=AtA5DbswJ90

Resolution notes covered in the Timbre and Tonality pass above: Kavakos's breath is audible, the violin's hall decay accompanies the main tone, and dynamic variation is well-rendered. The warmth of the tuning slightly reduces the exposed scratchiness of the bow on the strings compared to a strictly neutral IEM, but all the details that would satisfy careful, deliberate listening are present.

### ABC feat. Sophia Black by Polyphia

https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Mtd24QIBJ5Y

The IEM tames this chaotic track quite well. In cursory listening, without paying close attention, the chaotic whole is heard in its full glory without anything overly mushing over other parts. When slowing down and paying attention, any individual part of the track can be identified and traced, including the faint overdub and not just that.

### Memory (Gundam Seed Destiny OST)

https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=PhJWAWqExQs

The sense of space and ambience is excellent. The instrumental elements sit far back, feeling like they come from outside the head on the front left and front right, while the sound effect stays in the middle, slightly at the front of my head. It would be an overstatement to say it feels like sitting in a room with speakers around me, but there is a clear bubble of sound all around my head with this track. It is not speaker-like, but it is immersive.

### Shadow of Baar Dau

## Assessment

### Non-sound aspects

- Comfortable enough for multi-hour listening sessions without needing a break. No pain points, no hot spots, no pressure buildup. Personally, I wish AFUL could keep the nozzles of their IEMs narrow like they used to design their previous IEMs up to the Performer 7, but the larger nozzles are in line with other IEM designs and not offensive.
- Noise isolation is average. Not leaking sound in either direction, but nothing more than other IEMs with vents.
- The original ear tips are a good fit, so I did not need to roll initially. The usual recommendation still applies: Divinus Velvet tips (soft cap and wide opening). I usually go for wide opening to fully preserve the treble all the way to the ear canal, but I am surprised AFUL chose a more narrow opening for their stock ear tips. Whatever, it works. The only potential issue is that the stock tips extend the length of the P8S nozzles a little, which could make the fit tricky for listeners with shallower ear canals. In that case, short and wide tips like Divinus Velvet could help.
- Tip rolling follow-up: the best in my collection for this IEM is the Eletech Baroque. They are shorter with soft cap, wide opening, and good seal. Because they are short, the whole nozzle-plus-tip assembly becomes shorter, so the IEM body sits flusher against the ear. The soundstage is a bit less spread out compared to the stock tips, but there is a small improvement in transparency and clarity and in the precision of instrument positioning within the stage, on top of an already good stock. Divinus Velvet Wide Bore did not do well. They did not play well with the shape of the IEM and did not seal, so the body and impact of bass notes were mostly gone. Sancai Wide bore (wide open, short, soft cap) also did a good job, with a similar effect to Eletech Baroque and could be a good replacement. Spinfit W1 (long, medium opening, stiff cap, bendable head) had the strongest isolation and made the tone slightly leaner and crisper, which I assume is some kind of resonance from the longer nozzles accentuating upper mid and lower treble in-ear. If you want to tweak Performer 8S to be more crisp, this could be the one. For some reason, the bass impact was not as good as the stock tips for me. Personally, Eletech Baroque for this IEM.
- Packaging and accessories: the packaging has finally been improved. The box feels more upmarket, even against their most expensive IEM in the lineup, the AFUL Dawn-X. I like this move, since it is quite an expensive product in the grand scheme of things. People inside the IEM hobby for long enough would call $400-ish lower midrange, but let's be honest, these are earphones for $400, which is a high-end consumer product and should be presented as such. The content of the packaging is actually less up-scaled than the box suggests. Everything needed is inside, as usual: a faux leather case, IEM cables, cleaning tool, three sets of ear tips. There are also stickers and silicone plugs to support the new tunable bass port system. The case is less fancy than the one that came with AFUL's top IEMs (Cantor and Dawn-X), which is fair enough. These cases are not that expensive if you want to buy one for yourself aftermarket anyway.

### Timbre and Tonality

### Timbre and Tonality

- Slightly warm with a neutral-leaning balance, more musical than strictly correct. Energy across the upper midrange and treble is present but sits a touch recessed compared to a strictly neutral IEM. Low-end has body without bleeding into the lower mid.
- Foreground elements (lead guitar, vocals, brass melody) come through clearly without overpowering the bass instruments. Bass instruments are easy to follow on attention, and have details rather than just hum.
- The piano is balanced. High notes cut through without harshness, the left hand is easy to follow and not overpowered by the highs, and the interplay between voices can be followed.
- Male vocals land with emotive warmth and richness, sitting at the upper edge of neutral-warm without becoming muffled. Female vocals are bright, vibrant, and clear without harshness.
- Zero harshness or sibilance on sibilant-suspect recordings.
- Strings and orchestra are rendered with body and authority. Cellos carry rumble and resonance, violin carries hall decay and breath, brass cuts through with presence and authoritative impact. The slight warmth reduces the exposed scratchiness of the violin compared to a strictly neutral IEM and slightly softens the cellos tone in some passages, but it reads more as artistic rule-bending than as a flaw.

### Bass and Dynamic

- Epic bass lines land with authoritative impact and excellent control. Crescendos are communicated clearly, bass attacks are clean and decisive without skimping on top-of-beat energy or decay, and bass notes carry rumble and grippy texture rather than a low hum.
- Drum kit lands with impactful kicks, clean attacks, and authoritative physical punch at the top of each beat. Bass guitar has good but not overbearing presence, with details audible on attention rather than a low hum.
- Yes. The IEM makes the music want to move. Tracks with strong rhythms land with a clear sense of drive and momentum.

### Resolution

- Yes on most material. On ABC the chaotic whole is heard cleanly without anything overly mushing, and any individual part can be picked out on attention. There is some softness in the busiest passages on Strength of a Thousand Men where elements clump together, and I am not sure if this is the warmth getting in the way or a ceiling of the IEM.
- Yes. Cellos carry the rumble and resonance of the body and the proper midrange tone without becoming thin. The slight warmth can make the cellos feel a touch muffled in some passages, but the effect is small.
- Micro details are present and rewarding. Violin hall decay, breath, dynamic variation, faint overdubs in busy mixes, and side-channel backing vocals all surface without effort. The slight warmth reduces the exposed scratchiness of the violin compared to a strictly neutral IEM, but does not erase the details.
- A/B against Blessing 2, Andromeda 2020, and Cantor on ABC and other tracks. Performer 8S has a clear edge over Blessing 2; even though Blessing 2 has a lean tone and a more exposed midrange, the Performer 8S surfaces more details and cleaner separation in the chaotic track. Against Andromeda 2020 the Performer 8S has a slight edge in nuances and details, but the two sit closer together than the Performer 8S vs Blessing 2 comparison; Andromeda 2020's tuning pushes the chaotic forward all at once, making it a bit more disorienting to follow. Against Cantor, Cantor has a leaner, more neutral tone so it is easier to hear more details by default; setting tonal differences aside and focusing on what can and cannot be cleanly resolved, Cantor still has more nuances and details, and the gap becomes clearer after each swap. The Performer 8S is quite close to Cantor and the tone of Cantor does the heavy lifting to push it forward anyway. Land in the 4 band, +0.5 to acknowledge the closeness to Cantor in A/B, so 4.5 on resolution. Not in Cantor's band yet.

### Spatial

- 3D positioning in complex recordings is a strength. Instruments sit at distinct left-right positions and at distinct depths, with some elements floating clearly above the head in dense mixes. The presentation feels like an immersive bubble around the head rather than a flat plane across the ears. The size of the bubble is not super exaggerated: it wraps closely around the head rather than projecting all the way into the room (which is very rare for an IEM or headphone to do anyway, but some can). The positioning of instruments within the bubble is done in an interesting, three-dimensional way, and the IEM can produce a clear out-of-head illusion when the recording calls for it.
- Spacious recordings land with a strong out-of-head presentation. Background elements feel realistically distant, more in front of the face than inside the head.
- FPS games were tested with recordings of CS:GO gameplay. Performance translates well to games. Distant gunshots and explosions are particularly impressive in terms of the sense of distance and location. Forward positioning of sound is okay, though I have never had anything as good as a two-speaker setup in this regard.
- Soundstage and imaging are in the same band as Cantor. It does an outstanding job, so 5/5 on this dimension (entry of the 5/5 band).

## Final Assessment

### Summary

The Performer 8S delivers a slightly warm, musical tuning with authoritative bass and dynamic performance and outstanding spatial capability. The warmth is the dominant tonality choice and reads as rule-bending rather than a flaw: it adds emotional body to vocals and strings, gives female vocals a rich musical quality without going harsh, and makes the bass attacks feel physical rather than analytical. The trade-off is real. The slight warmth reduces the exposed scratchiness of violin compared to a strictly neutral IEM like the Cantor, and on cellos it can make the tone feel a touch muffled in some passages. Across busy orchestral material the energy level reads slightly muted compared to a strictly neutral IEM, which will be a con for listeners after a brighter and more forward sound. The upper-mid and treble sit a touch recessed relative to neutral.

Bass and dynamic is where the IEM clearly lands at the top of its band. Crescendos are communicated with grin-inducing impact. Every beat carries grippy texture and rumble without smearing, and kick drums land with clean attacks and authoritative physical punch at the top of the note. Across busy passages the Performer 8S holds up well, though there is a softness in the loudest, most cluttered moments where elements clump together; whether this is the warmth getting in the way or a performance ceiling is not fully clear.

Soundstage and imaging sit at Cantor's level: three-dimensional positioning inside a tight, immersive bubble around the head, with a clear out-of-head illusion when the recording calls for it. The bubble wraps closely around the head rather than projecting all the way into the room (a rare trick for any IEM or headphone), but the positioning of instruments within that bubble is precise and, in dense mixes, some elements float clearly above the head. Spacious recordings land with background elements feeling realistically distant, more in front of the face than inside the head. The game translation confirms the same impression: distant gunshots and explosions carry a convincing sense of distance and location, with forward positioning good but not at the level of a two-speaker setup.

Resolution is high: a clear step up from Blessing 2, a slight step up from Andromeda 2020, just below Cantor, with micro-details surfacing on attention. The Tidal 24-bit / 96 kHz versus YouTube comparison showed the gap between source qualities is more audible than usual on this IEM, so redbook 44.1 kHz at minimum is recommended as a source quality floor.

Comfortable for multi-hour sessions with no pain points or pressure buildup, though I wish AFUL had kept the narrower nozzle profile of their older IEMs. Noise isolation is average for a vented IEM. The official silicone tips work well and are a good fit out of the box. Eletech Baroque is the recommended roll for listeners who want a small additional gain in transparency, clarity, and precision of instrument positioning; the trade-off is that the soundstage becomes slightly less spread out, because the shorter tip brings the IEM body flush against the ear. Divinus Velvet Wide Bore did not seal well on this shell; Spinfit W1 tightened isolation and crisped the tone but reduced bass impact; Sancai Wide bore is a good Eletech Baroque alternative. Packaging has been upgraded to a more upmarket box, but the case is less fancy than the Cantor and Dawn-X, which is a fair concession at this price.

### Overall thought

The Performer 8S is the follow-up to the original Performer 8, which was the IEM that put AFUL on my radar because it was the first mid-fi IEM to convincingly land in my 4/5 band on resolution. That was a big deal at the time, since most mid-fi IEMs back then, even the $600 to $700 ones, barely cleared my 3/5 on resolution. The original Performer 8 was, however, flawed on the dynamic and bass front: clean, neutral, very detailed, but not punchy, snappy, or dynamic enough, leading to a detailed but rather flat experience. The soundstage of the original Performer 8 was also rather flat, though precise enough.

Following up on such an IEM is tricky. By 2025, the IEM market had jumped significantly to the point that the level of detail of the original Performer 8 is no longer a wow factor but somewhat an expectation. So I was curious, and a bit concerned, about how AFUL would follow up.

How did they do with the Performer 8S? I think it is both great and not great for some listeners. The great thing is that they managed to push the technical performance further on all fronts, especially bass quality, dynamic, and stereo imaging, without losing the resolution. For the first time, a mid-fi IEM gives me a total package that stands up to my scrutiny in focused listening sessions, works well as background music, and never makes me feel like something is still missing. I do not feel the urge to reach for my higher-end IEMs. In this sense, the Performer 8S is a perfect follow-up to the original Performer 8, and dare I say, one of if not the best IEM AFUL has designed so far.

But there is another angle. The Performer 8S is a different IEM from the original Performer 8. The original Performer 8 is crisp, clean, flat, and precise. The Performer 8S is tuned for warmth, bassy, cozy, enjoyable. If a listener is expecting exactly the same IEM as the original Performer 8 but better, they could be severely disappointed by the change. For these listeners, the AFUL Cantor is still the only true upgrade to the original Performer 8 they can get.

There are also some concerns. The midrange is warmer than usual, which could lead to a muffled sensation on a few tracks. AFUL has a particular way of tuning the midrange that pushes the region around 800 to 1250 Hz earlier than other IEMs, so the first moment with an AFUL IEM could be somewhat strange if you are coming fresh from a usual IEM that has later ear-gain compensation tuning. Despite my praise for the bass, this is not a bass-head IEM. It just takes whatever bass there is in the track, pushes it a little, and reproduces it well. It does not make bass the dominant part of the track.

All in all, I can recommend this IEM as a jack-of-all-trades, everyday-carry, "nice" IEM in your collection. I do, because I use it so in my own setup. The thing is, the Performer 8S is not the bright shining star like the original Performer 8 was, in my view. The original Performer 8 was the first one to break through. Now, the Performer 8S is more in line with what I expect from a mid-fi IEM in 2026. It is not a fault of AFUL, but it shows how much the IEM market has changed and how fast chifi IEM technology has improved. For the consumer, I guess it is a win. The Performer 8S is as good as if not better than any kilobuck-class IEM I have ever tried or wanted to buy, and I do not feel like I want to pursue a kilobuck anymore. But like I said, it is not just the Performer 8S that reaches there. The market is reaching there.

### Recommendation

Recommended as a jack-of-all-trades, everyday-carry, "nice" IEM in your collection. I use it this way in my own setup, and it works well across focused listening sessions and as background music. It is as good as if not better than any kilobuck-class IEM I have tried or wanted to buy, and I do not feel the urge to pursue a kilobuck anymore, though the market as a whole is reaching that level rather than the Performer 8S alone.

Skip if you are coming from the original Performer 8 expecting exactly the same IEM but better. The Performer 8S is a meaningfully different tuning (warm, bassy, cozy) from the original (crisp, clean, flat, precise). For an upgrade to the original Performer 8 in the same house style, the AFUL Cantor remains the true upgrade path.

Also note: not a bass-head IEM. It just takes whatever bass is in the track and reproduces it well; it does not make bass the dominant part of the track.
