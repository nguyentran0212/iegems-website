# HiBy RS8 II assessment

## Opening

**Product under test:** HiBy RS8 II sample unit

**Listening gear:**
- AFUL Cantor
- AFUL Dawn-X
- AFUL Performer8S
- Campfire Audio Andromeda 2020
- Sennheiser HD 560S

**Baseline sources:**
- Luxury & Precision W4 dongle
- HiBy R6 Pro Max DAP
- iBasso DX300 DAP

**Output settings:** All relevant output settings will be tested. Selected tracks will be used to compare the differences between configurations.

**Music library:** Lossless FLAC test library and Tidal at Max quality.

**Firmware version:** 1.50_20260106-1502

**Caveats and DSP settings:** The AI tone mimic feature will be disabled for most of the assessment. All EQ will be disabled.

## Listening Test

Setup note: baselines are rotated deliberately across tracks to generate richer, more varied comparison data rather than repeating the same A/B pairing. Tracks 1–8: FD5 (class-A). Tracks 9–11: R6 Pro Max.

### Shivers by Ed Sheeran

https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=z2_Lrg6rRks

For this one, I tested using the AFUL Cantor (very neutral IEM, without cut back at 6kHz, so if the track is harsh, like this one, it would show the full scale of pain). Since it's hard to talk about an audio source like a DAP in isolation, I also brought in my HiBy FD5, which is a desktop all-in-one (DAC+amp) device sharing some of the technology from the HiBy R8II, the delta-sigma flagship of the line (separate product: R = delta-sigma, RS = R2R, 8 = top tier, II = mark II; the FD5 shares the R8II amp stage but has a simpler DAC [not 100% confirmed]). I set the FD5 in its class-A amplifier mode. In A/B testing, it was quite surprising to me that the RS8 II has a very slight edge in clarity and details, but at the same time is harsher in the treble. Meanwhile, the FD5 is slightly less clear, less detailed, but at the same time easier on the ears. The surprising finding from this track is that, contrary to the general assumption of the audiophile community that R2R DAC is more "mellow", the RS8 II was actually the one with more clarity and sharpness. If I just think about the RS8 II on its own, I would say it has done what I would expect from it with this track. It's a harshly mixed track, and I'm using a very revealing and detailed IEM with unforgiving treble, so the harshness is to be expected. But the midrange was good, Ed's voice was detailed. The stage was well constructed. It performs as I would expect, though the gap vs lesser audio source devices was not as apparent as I would like with this particular track.

### Kiwi wa Boku ni Niteiru by See-Saw

https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=U6BkF23UPbM

Same setup, AFUL Cantor with the FD5 for A/B. The finding follows the surprise from the previous track. Again, the RS8 II makes the lead singer's voice noticeably clearer, more vibrant, and more detailed, without any thinness or harshness or any weirdness in the tone. It's just outstandingly detailed and a nice rendition. Meanwhile, the FD5 feels a bit "cloudy" in direct comparison. However, the FD5 does not lose completely. For some reason, the FD5 edges out in terms of the spatial effect in this particular track, particularly the first 30 seconds. With the FD5, I had a sort of "wow" moment with the distant instruments at the forward left of the soundstage feeling like they convincingly pop out of the headspace. The RS8 II did convey good soundstage size, but it did not give me that "wow". I tried again and again at different volume levels, but it is what it is.

### Now We Are Free by 2CELLOS

https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=74CYIdYoQ5w

I must say the RS8 II does a spectacular job here. It renders the tone of the cellos just right, and the bass and the beat from the orchestra in this track is rendered top notch. It renders this track with authority. The combo of the RS8 II and Cantor was mesmerising that when I finished the track, I had to rewind it for another listen. What impresses me the most was the clarity and control across the spectrum. In this case, it was not like forced clarity with a treble boost. It's more like, even when the complex arrangement has multiple string instruments occupying similar spectrum, the RS8 II still helps the Cantor pull them apart clearly, so it's just so easy and clear to hear everything. In comparison, the FD5 does have a larger illusion of soundstage in this track, but it was also more "cloudy" in comparison, so it's definite that it is the RS8 II, not just because the Cantor can do a good job on its own.

### Playing God by Polyphia

https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Z5NoQg8LdDk

The RS8 II does an incredible job again here. The star of the show, to me, is the clarity and control across the spectrum. It makes this complex track so much easier to dissect, and thus makes it more mesmerising. It's like everything is crisp and clear and easy to track, even the small details in the cymbals or hi hats. Another win is the control over the bass information, particularly the texture and details of the bass guitar. The direct A/B against the FD5 highlights how far ahead the clarity and control of the RS8 II is. Again, this is not to bash the FD5. On its own, it's a pretty strong performer. There is a reason why I put it on my desk, after all.

### Bach's Goldberg Variations, recorded by Lang Lang

https://youtu.be/linQUK6jNmg

No surprise here. The RS8 II renders the piano excellent, with no tonality issue to mention. And again, the RS8 II does a remarkable job on control, refinement, and fine details across the spectrum. The piano voices are crystal clear, well separated, and sound real.

### Ciaccona from Bach's Violin Partita No. 2 in D Minor, performed by Kavakos

https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=AtA5DbswJ90

Again, no surprise. The RS8 II renders the violin tone how it's supposed to be. And again, it excels when it comes to fine details of the violin. Simply put, the background feels void black, so that only the pure violin tone and the reverberation of the background and breath of Kavakos pop up with purity and clarity. It's mesmerising for me due to the clarity and detail, and in this particular track, the sense of space thanks to the excellent reproduction of reverberation. I had to tear myself away from the track to move on to the next test track.

### Synchro BOM-BA-YE by Tokyo Kosei Wind Orchestra

https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=4lwPiXnOGZ4

The RS8 II, again, no surprise, and as expected, does an excellent job with this track. The details that it squeezes out with the Cantor while remaining natural, not harsh, not forced, is impressive. There is no issue for me to report regarding tonality. I also find the pitch black background of the RS8 II plays nicely with these sorts of orchestral tracks. To put in context, I again compare against the FD5. I hear a somewhat larger soundstage but also a more blurry stereo image. One might argue that the FD5 rendition would sound more like "real life", whilst the crisp and detailed rendition of the RS8 II would sound more like a "studio recording". I think you need to pick your poison in this case. Personally, I lean towards the crisp and detail of the RS8 II. It just makes the track catch my attention more.

### A Reckoning in Blood from The Ghost of Tsushima OST

https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=kaBMXXo-vdU

Three things stood out for me in how the RS8 II handles this track. First, again, it's very crisp and precise across the soundstage. Everything is clear and detailed, from the distant background elements to the nuances in the drum sound of the beat. Second, the sense of locality, especially background elements of the track, is reproduced quite phenomenally with the RS8 II, maybe due to the clarity and details. It's like these elements are convincingly placed in the surrounding environment. Third, the centre of the soundstage of the RS8 II is actually quite a bit closed in. It's like there is a strong, clear "foreground" that feels like what I would expect from an IEM, but then there is that holographic background effect that brings back the sense of space. This precision translates nicely to the attack of the drum. Each drum hit also has a great sense of power. I feel that it is the precision, snappy tactility that carries the sense of rhythm and power and dynamic of this track with the RS8 II, rather than the pure powerful thump. To put in context, I compare and contrast against the FD5 again. For the FD5, again, I hear the foreground to take a step or two back, so everything feels more spread out vs the RS8 II. But at the same time, everything is a bit blurrier and a bit less incisive. Similar observation in terms of the bass quality. The FD5 feels slightly more thumpy, but at the same time less incisive and snappy than the RS8 II.

### Strength of a Thousand Men (Live) by Two Steps from Hell

https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=POmTXTBtRZA

For this track, I use the HiBy R6 Pro Max, a mid-range delta-sigma DAP from HiBy's current line, as the comparison baseline. The same IEM (AFUL Cantor) is used as the reference. When I listen to this track with the RS8 II, there is a sense of power and focus, on top of the expected clarity and details. It's very easy to hear individual details in the bassline, but it does not mean that they are weak or wimpy and focused only on the clarity. Simply put, there is "meat on the bone" of the bass note, with a clear sense of sub-bass (I can feel a tiny tactile "kick" at the nape of my head). The soundstage, again, concentrates around the middle, but with the background part of the stage "popping out" into the surrounding environment in a holographic way. Switching to the R6 Pro Max reveals a much more significant difference than I expected. Immediately, I felt the stage to be noticeably wider but also more diffused and flatter than the RS8 II. The R6 Pro Max somewhat keeps up with the clarity and incisiveness of the RS8 II, but its bassline lacks the depth and power that the RS8 II provides. For this particular track and this particular IEM, I would say the RS8 II presents a noticeably more powerful and satisfying experience. I would need to EQ up the lower midrange of the R6 Pro Max a touch or swap to a different IEM for this particular track.

### Ciaccona from Bach's Violin Partita No. 2 in D Minor, performed by Kavakos (resolution)

https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=AtA5DbswJ90

For this track, I use the AFUL Cantor as the reference IEM and the R6 Pro Max DAP as the reference for comparison again. Again, the RS8 II reveals noticeably more nuances and details in this track. In fact, I was a bit surprised by the gain in details, as the R6 Pro Max has been my daily driving DAP for a long time, and I have never felt like I miss anything from the track, so I was taken aback when comparing the same track with the same IEM side by side. But the RS8 II pulled ahead in this track not just by the details. It also offers a different tonal balance that is warmer, making the violin sound richer. The reverberation of the stage is also more fully represented. The R6 Pro Max, in comparison, has a colder tone and a wider but flatter soundstage.

### ABC feat. Sophia Black by Polyphia

https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Mtd24QIBJ5Y

For this track, I compare with the R6 Pro Max again, using the Cantor. There is no surprise that needs to be emphasised again here. The RS8 II again has a slightly warmer tone and noticeably more crystal clear separation between layers in the mix, as well as a higher level of details in each layer, especially the background distant ambience sounds. At the same time, it has a more compact, more concentrated soundstage presentation that focuses on the middle, rather than spreading out. In comparison, the R6 Pro Max sounds flatter, both in terms of the shape of the soundstage and the tonality.

### Original Sound Effect Track - Memory from Gundam Seed Destiny OST (soundstage and imaging)

https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=PhJWAWqExQs

For this track, I used various IEMs to test the pairing compatibility of the RS8 II. First, the Campfire Audio Andromeda 2020, a notoriously sensitive IEM that easily reveals background hiss or gets too loud. With the RS8 II, I had to use low-gain and set the volume to 20% with this track. On the plus side, there is no hiss, and I still have some range for adjusting volume further down. In terms of sound quality, the RS8 II does an excellent job of enhancing the strengths (the holographic stereo imaging ability and details) and minimising the weaknesses (thin, not impactful BA bass) of the Andromeda. Again, the soundstage is not very spread out, but it has a realistic ambience and very 3D positioning, making this track very engaging. For the next IEM, I used the AFUL Dawn-X. This is a flagship IEM that is moderately difficult to drive well. However, the main reason I chose this is to test the pairing of the RS8 II with a more mellow IEM. I wondered whether the mellow nature of the IEM would work well with the slightly warmer and thicker tone of the RS8 II. It turns out that there is nothing to be concerned about. The treble of the Dawn-X is fully presented by the RS8 II, helping the IEM to project an immersive soundstage in this track. The elements on the side channels of the track are particularly impressive, as they sound like they come from small speakers outside my head, on the side. Again, everything is crystal clear and easy to dissect down to the minute details. No surprise here. Though I am a bit surprised that the Dawn-X requires less volume on the RS8 II than the Cantor. For the next test, I switch to the Sennheiser HD560S headphone. For reference, I must admit that I never enjoy this headphone: too tight, and too much upper midrange, it sounds thin, loud, and shrill, and also flattens the soundstage. Unfortunately, the RS8 II cannot do anything to improve this situation, despite how it seems to be able to assert some real performance-bending capability when working with IEMs. The stage still feels flat, and the tuning still feels too loud and shrill, and the clamp force is still squeezing my head. The only improvements I can think of are the extra details that the RS8 II manages to pull out of the HD560S, and perhaps the attack of the bass notes is a little bit more incisive, but it is not a worthwhile trade-off, in my opinion.

### Shadow of Baar Dau

https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=RXcLuLQ_5oM

For this track, I focus on comparing the sound quality of the RS8 II in different audio settings instead. The IEM for testing is the Cantor again. In terms of the stereo imaging and soundstage, there is no more surprise to talk about at this point. The RS8 II conveys a great sense of ambience and depth, as expected. In terms of gain level: I would say the sound is, as expected, identical across gain levels with only two differences: loudness and noise. When I move from low to medium gain, I am surprised to hear a very faint but consistent hiss in the background that was not present at low gain. Increasing to high gain further increases this hiss. It is not a disruptive, loud, obnoxious hissing, but if you pay attention, you will hear it, especially in comparison against low gain. So in this case, I do not see the need for increasing gain to get some extra perceived dynamic. You lose more than you gain here. Turbo Mode: similar to the R8 II, the RS8 II also has a "Turbo Mode". Noted that it is not a gain level above high gain. In fact, when I turn on Turbo Mode, the volume level remains identical. But the presentation changes quite a bit in two ways. First, the soundstage is projected further out. Notice how I kept mentioning that the stage is holographic but does not spread far out from the head? With Turbo Mode on, the stage changes in that way. The second change is in terms of the power of the bass note. Not louder drums, but it is like the drum packs a bit more sub-bass power. Simply put, Turbo Mode makes the RS8 II feel more powerful. But it comes with a warning of increased power drain and heat generation. Amplifier modes: the RS8 II can switch between class AB, class A, and an adaptive mode that dynamically switches between the two based on incoming transients in the music. Right off the bat, I do not really like the class AB. The stage feels noticeably flatter and in-my-face. It just feels a bit dull. Switching to class A gives me back the depth and thickness of the bass line that I prefer. The adaptive mode sounds almost indistinguishable from the manual class A mode. HiBy claims that adaptive mode would reduce heat and energy consumption, leading to better battery life. I did not have the means to test this claim, unfortunately. For the low-pass filter on the DAC, I cannot say that I can clearly hear the difference between these in my tests, so I left the DAC at the Darwin default filter. For the harmonic controller, I can hear a more noticeable difference. Turning this all the way up makes the sound, particularly higher notes, a bit more "fuzzy", which could give a more comfy vibe. But it is a small difference, unlike my previous experience with previous R2R DAPs from HiBy like the RS6. For my listening preference, I would pick low gain, Turbo Mode, adaptive amplifier mode, and leave the DAC at default settings.

## Assessment

### Non-sound aspects

- **Construction and design:** Well constructed, with interesting edges and angles. Not a generic rectangular slab, but a piece of precise engineering artwork. Nothing about the device seems generic or simple in terms of machining, even down to small details. Instead of simple rectangular buttons, it has hexagonal buttons. Instead of a simple rectangular SD card flap, it has a more angular, metal flap that sits flush with the rest of the device, both physically and aesthetically. The volume knob at the top is beautifully designed and machined. Even the ports for USB-C and mini HDMI are machined into the shell of the device, rather than just a cut-out like usual. The only area to complain about is the massive chunk of empty space at the top next to the volume knob. It feels like HiBy ran out of ideas after finishing the rest of the design. The left edge is also empty but does not stand out as much. There is a beautiful design element at the back of the device, but this is hidden in day-to-day use when the device is in the included leather case. Weight is lighter than expected. It is lighter than the midrange R6 Pro Max, and substantially lighter than its predecessor, the RS8 (Mark 1). But at the same time, it is still a hunk of metal.
- **Screen:** Average in the grand scheme of things, since it is just an LCD panel. It means it is not true black like OLED, and there is some light bleed at the upper edge of the screen. But at the same time, this is a damn fine LCD screen. It is sharp, vivid, and contrasty. Most of the time the screen would be off, as it is a music player, not a Nintendo Switch. But when using the screen to watch video, there is nothing to complain about. One trade-off: the screen size is slightly smaller than the daily driver R6 Pro Max. But the panel is slightly more vivid, so it is a fair trade-off.
- **Latency:** No latency on wired connections, of course. The Bluetooth side is not as good. Tested by using the HiBy FD5 as the LDAC receiver. When streaming a YouTube video from the RS8 II, audio latency was immediately noticeable and unpleasant. The first video tested was an interview with drivers after an F1 race, and the voice was noticeably desynced from the lips. The latency felt worse with piano recordings, and completely off with drum covers. So for watching video over Bluetooth, this is not a good device. But to be fair, why would you use Bluetooth when you have the kick-ass onboard audio hardware of the RS8 II?
- **Software experience:** What you would expect. It is essentially just an Android phone that swaps the cellular connection hardware for audio hardware. The big software effort done by HiBy is hidden deep inside the OS to ensure that the audio hardware is utilised properly. At the user interface level, it is just a raw, zero-bloat Android experience. And it is very smooth, courtesy of the Snapdragon QCS8550 SoC (essentially a Snapdragon 8 Gen 2 without the cellular hardware), 16 GB of RAM, and 512 GB of internal storage. This is the same chip powering some of the latest Android retro handheld emulators like the Retroid Pocket Nova. It means if you want to play games on this device, you can easily play most of the Switch library, and even older PC titles from the 2010s at respectable frame rates. Yes, Android devices can run real PC games natively now, in 2026. You can attach a telescopic game controller to this device, but the best way is to video out to a screen and use a game controller via Bluetooth. It is a perfectly good way to get more use out of your expensive DAP. The audio settings side is where HiBy delivers the most: hardware controls including amplifier modes, gain, Turbo Mode, and DAC settings; EQ including a 10-band parametric EQ plus HiBy MSEB (essentially parametric EQ with pre-defined bands to adjust subjective experiences such as note thickness, sibilance, and bass texture); and DSP plugins covering everything from crossfeed to improving soundstage to making transients more dynamic. Besides the usual ones, the RS8 II also has something new called Sankofa AI. It uses the onboard tensor cores to run a neural network to emulate the tone of vintage devices. Out of the box, it ships with profiles for eight minidisc players. Turning on this emulation layer immediately changes the sound profile of the RS8 II, and each profile sounds distinctive. Unfortunately, there is no experience or nostalgia with minidisc, so this feature does not personally resonate. The childhood was more about cassette tape and radio on Chinese knock-offs of Sony players than fancy minidisc. Also, the old-is-better argument does not apply here, as the sound quality took quite a hit with this emulation layer on. There is a market for this feature, but personally, a more interesting use of the tensor cores would be running some sort of stereo image improvement to make the sound even more immersive.

### Timbre and Tonality

- **Overall signature:** Neutral-warm. The word I keep coming back to is "mature." Not overdone, but just enough deviation from flat to make the presentation engaging without ever becoming coloured or artificial. Against the colder, more clinical R6 Pro Max, the RS8 II is warmer. Against a warm delta-sigma device like the FD5, the RS8 II is actually slightly more neutral and transparent. The void-black background is the thread running through every track. Instruments and voices breathe in their own space, decays decay cleanly, and micro details surface naturally rather than being spotlighted.
- **Band balance:** Even and even-handed. On complex tracks like Playing God, each instrument stays distinct and easy to track. Nothing gets buried or emphasised at the expense of another.
- **Piano balance:** Even across the frequency range on the Goldberg Variations. No register is tipped or recessed.
- **Vocals:** Vivid, clear, and detailed. On Shivers, Ed Sheeran's voice is detailed with good midrange body. On the See-Saw track, the lead vocalist is bright and clear without thinness.
- **Sibilance:** Natural and controlled. Shivers is a harsh recording and with the unforgiving Cantor the RS8 II manages the harshness appropriately without suppressing the vocal.
- **Strings and orchestra:** Thick and authoritative on cellos, pure and detailed on violins with excellent reverb tails. No congestion or muddiness on any orchestral track.

### Bass and Dynamic

- **Epic bass lines:** Strong, precise, and controlled. The sense of power comes from snappy, incisive attack and convincing sub-bass texture, not from sheer thump.
- **Drum kit and bass guitar:** Each drum hit has great power and tactile attack. Bass guitar texture and detail are standout, even in complex arrangements. No congestion.
- **Toe-tapping:** Yes. Every beat is snappy and incisive on tracks like Strength of a Thousand Men. The marching energy is fully conveyed.

### Resolution

- **Complex overlapping recordings:** Outstanding. Nothing crumbled or congested. Every layer stays distinct and easy to follow.
- **Congested lower frequencies:** Handled well. The void-black background prevents mush. Heavy low-frequency content stays clean and controlled.
- **Micro details:** Excellent. Bow catches on strings, performer breath, reverb decay tails, hi-hat nuances all surface naturally and cleanly.

### Spatial

- **3D positioning:** Centred and holographic rather than wide. Strong, clear foreground with a convincing 3D depth. Elements appear convincingly placed in the surrounding environment rather than spread flatly across a plane.
- **Sense of space:** Convincing and immersive. The void-black background lets reverb and decay breathe naturally. Side channel imaging projects outside the head like small speakers.
- **FPS game sound positioning:** Precise. Tested on CS:GO gameplay recordings, the RS8 II performs as expected, constrained mainly by the IEM. Turbo Mode pushes sounds further from the head, making localisation easier.

### Pairing / Compatibility

- **Highly sensitive IEMs:** No hiss on the Andromeda 2020 at 20% volume on low gain. Sufficient volume adjustment range downward.
- **Difficult-to-drive IEMs:** Excellent. The Dawn-X drove well, requiring less volume than the Cantor despite being a more demanding IEM. No audible difference in drive quality compared to the FD5 desktop reference.

### Selected Comparisons

- **vs RS8 Mark 1:** The RS8 II is an all-around improvement. The biggest improvement is the tonality and timbre. The original RS8 sounds "off," in a way that is hard to put a finger on. It is not just some arbitrary R2R tone or anything like that, since the original RS8 was not a warm-tuned DAP either. It just does not work well with these ears. And when the tone is not quite right, it is hard to get into more detailed comparison on other aspects, since the tonal issue keeps standing out like a sore thumb. The Mark 2 fixes this entirely. Also worth noting: the RS8 II is significantly lighter than the Mark 1. Still a hunk of metal, but not as backbreaking.
- **vs R8 II:** No longer available for direct comparison. The R8 II is the delta-sigma sibling of the RS8 II.

## Final Assessment

### Summary

**Overview:** The RS8 II is an R2R flagship DAP that sounds, to my ears, fundamentally right. Across all tracks and all comparisons, it consistently performs at the level I expect from a top-tier source. It never gets in the way of the music, and it never gets boring.

**Tone:** The tonality is neutral-warm. The word I keep coming back to is "mature." Not overdone, but just enough deviation from flat to make the presentation engaging without ever becoming coloured or artificial. The void-black background is the thread running through every track. Instruments and voices breathe in their own space, decays decay cleanly, and micro details surface naturally rather than being spotlighted.

**Resolution:** Resolution is outstanding, and it is the most consistent strength across every comparison. The RS8 II pulls apart overlapping layers and retrieves micro details with an ease that makes the FD5 and R6 Pro Max both sound cloudy and flat in direct comparison. Even after daily-driving the R6 Pro Max for years and never feeling like I was missing anything, the RS8 II revealed more nuance on the same tracks with the same IEM.

**Bass:** Bass and dynamics are precise and controlled rather than raw and visceral. The sense of power comes from snappy, incisive attack and convincing sub-bass texture, not from sheer thump. On the right tracks, the tactile quality of the bass carries the rhythm and the sense of scale simultaneously. Turbo Mode adds a touch more sub-bass depth without making the drums louder.

**Soundstage:** The soundstage is centred and holographic. Strong foreground focus with a convincing 3D depth that projects outward rather than spreading flatly across a wide plane. It is not the widest stage in absolute terms, but it is spatially coherent and immersive. Turbo Mode expands the stage further outward, which is the single most impactful output setting change available.

**Software and sound settings:** The user interface is a raw, zero-bloat Android experience. Performance is silky smooth courtesy of the Snapdragon QCS8550 SoC, 16 GB RAM, and 512 GB storage, and it is capable of running games and video output as secondary use cases. The audio settings underneath are extensive: amplifier modes, gain levels, Turbo Mode, DAC low-pass filters, a 10-band parametric EQ plus HiBy MSEB, and DSP plugins covering crossfeed, soundstage enhancement, and transient control. The Sankofa AI feature uses onboard tensor cores to emulate vintage minidisc player tones. It sounds distinctive but comes with a sound quality trade-off, and it does not appeal to me personally.

**Optimal recommended settings:** Low gain, Turbo Mode on, adaptive amplifier mode, Darwin default low-pass filter, harmonic controller to taste, Sankofa AI off.

### Overall thought

The RS8 II is very expensive. But at the same time, it is one of the best listening experiences I have ever tested, across both official reviews and casual tests. Simply put, it pulls more details and depth from familiar IEMs that I did not expect to be even possible. And it does so with a great sense of maturity and refinement. The RS8 II does not give the immediate "wow, what a huge soundstage" like its sibling the R8 II, but it is more about sitting down, giving it time, and then before I knew it, a huge chunk of time has gone by and I still do not want to stop. The tonality, timbre, and the outstanding clarity and details form a potent combination. And when Turbo Mode is on, any of my reservations about the soundstage are also gone.

### Recommendation

Again, this device is very, very expensive. I do not recommend this device for listeners who are just starting their IEM journey, even if one has money to burn. I think one needs to experience quite a few different DAP and IEM combinations to fully appreciate the refinement this DAP brings. I also do not recommend this device for headphone listeners. I mean, it could work, but I do not have one of those current intensive planar headphones to test, and my hunch is that the amplifier might not have spare power to pull everything out of those headphones. So who is this device for? I would say for listeners like me: 99% listening to IEM, loving a good tone, details, and immersive soundstage. I know I would miss this device, and would keep an eye on the used market for one day.
